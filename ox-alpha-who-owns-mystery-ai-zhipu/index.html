Qui possède Ox Alpha ? Enquête sur l'IA Mystère Dépassant Claude et GPT

On August 20, 2026, an unnamed AI model appeared on OpenRouter with no press release, no corporate branding, and no creator. Within days, it climbed to the top of every usage chart, outperforming GPT-5 and Grok 4 on complex coding tasks. The internet gave it a name: Ox Alpha. Millions of developers and researchers began asking the same question: who made this? We conducted a deep OSINT investigation — combining WHOIS forensics, DNS analysis, tokenizer fingerprinting, error-code tracing, and corporate registry searches across multiple countries — to answer that question. Here is what we found.

The Two Layers of Mystery

The first trap in this investigation is conflating the website with the model. They are controlled by completely different entities.

Layer 1: oxalpha.com — The Anonymous Wrapper

The website oxalpha.com appeared almost simultaneously with the model, offering a free, no-login chat interface. Our WHOIS investigation revealed:

The domain was originally a speculative registration — likely parked since 2022 — then reactivated in August 2026 to capitalize on the buzz. The site's own About page confirms the separation:
"oxalpha.com is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or operated by whoever built the model. We're enthusiasts who wanted a fast, free way to use it."
Its Terms of Use contain no company name, no registration number, and a deliberately vague jurisdiction clause. No match was found on OpenCorporates, Companies House (UK), or any corporate registry. Verdict: The website is a third-party fan project. Not the creator.

Layer 2: The AI Model — Digital Forensics

If the website is a dead end, how do you identify the real creator? The same way digital forensics experts catch criminals: by examining fingerprints. Every AI model carries unique signatures — in its tokenizer, error messages, and multimodal pipeline — that are as identifiable as human DNA.

Evidence #1: Identical Tokenizer (95/95 Match)

Researcher Joseph Elstner, using the open-source modelprint tool, ran Ox Alpha through 95 probe tests comparing its tokenizer against known model families. The result: 95 out of 95 perfect matches with Zhipu AI's GLM-5 family. A systematic +75 token offset per prompt was detected, attributed to a hidden system prompt wrapper — a known Zhipu engineering practice.

Evidence #2: Proprietary Error Codes

When Ox Alpha encounters invalid parameters, it returns error codes 1210 (invalid reasoning_effort) and 1214 (internal configuration error). These codes are documented exclusively in Zhipu AI's official API documentation. No other AI provider in the world uses these identifiers.

Evidence #3: Video Processing Pipeline

Ox Alpha's multimodal video encoding — how it segments and consumes video tokens per frame — is an exact match with the GLM-5V-Turbo pipeline, including identical per-frame token budgets.

Evidence #4: The "Pony Alpha" Precedent

Zhipu AI previously deployed a model under the anonymous codename "Pony Alpha" using the exact same playbook: an "Alpha" name, no attribution, third-party platform distribution, and free unlimited access during stealth testing. The pattern is identical.

Why a Stealth Launch?

The strategy is deliberate. By deploying GLM-5.3 Flash anonymously, Zhipu AI achieves three goals:
  • Unbiased Real-World Benchmarks: Without a brand attached, testers judge the model on merit alone — not reputation.
  • Organic Viral Buzz: Mystery generates more media coverage than any traditional press release.
  • Production Stress Testing: Free unlimited access during stealth means millions of diverse real-world prompts — the ultimate quality assurance pipeline.

If history follows the "Pony Alpha" precedent, an official announcement from Z.AI could come within days or weeks.
